Morse Reference

1. English Morse (International Standard)

The standard Morse code, internationally agreed since 1865. Every Latin letter and digit has a fixed pattern that is used all over the world.

2. Arabic Morse (MC88 Custom)

Arabic never had an official Morse standard. I designed this one from scratch around a single rule: the more frequent a letter is in Arabic, the shorter its code. This mirrors the principle Samuel Morse used in 1835 for the Latin alphabet.

The table is sorted from most frequent to least frequent. The first two letters get one symbol. The next four get two. The next eight get three. The next sixteen get four. The final letter gets five.

3. Design Rules

  • Frequency-based codes. More frequent letters receive shorter codes.
  • Hamza normalization. أ، إ، آ are encoded as ا. ؤ is encoded as و. ئ is encoded as ي. Standalone hamza (ء) has its own code.
  • Diacritics are ignored. Tashkeel (fatḥa, ḍamma, kasra, shadda, sukūn) does not affect the Morse output.
  • Numbers and punctuation use the international standard, so the system stays compatible with standard Morse for those.
  • Word separation. A forward slash "/" separates words. A regular space separates letters.

4. Sources

The frequency ordering is based on approximate values reported in common Modern Standard Arabic corpora:

  • Penn Arabic Treebank
  • King Saud University Arabic Corpus
  • ArabiCorpus

Frequency values differ slightly across corpora, so the exact ordering is approximate. The general shape — ا and ل first, ظ and غ last — is consistent across sources.
